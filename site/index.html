Why it exists

Life changes fast. Paperwork doesn't keep up.

Family caregivers juggle calls, visits, emails, portals and letters across offices that don't talk to each other. When something doesn't add up, the answer is usually already somewhere in your own phone. It's just scattered. phone-net pulls it into one timeline you can search, so you can ask clear questions in writing.

Why caregivers get lost

The lines are tangled

Company A sets the care plan. Company B runs payroll. The caregiver calls both. Each points at the other, and the person receiving care has to fight just to let the caregiver speak for her.

What we learned doing it

Getting complex systems to talk, one plain step at a time

Worked out the hard way, by a family caregiver, on very little sleep.

  1. Gather everything you already own. Phone audio, SD cards, email, scanned letters and bills. Pull only what's new; verify every copy.
  2. Fix the dates. Backups stamp files with the day they were copied. The audio itself usually knows when it was recorded.
  3. Hear each voice. Level quiet speech, transcribe, and separate who's talking, so the TV isn't mistaken for a person.
  4. Search by word and by meaning. Your own filenames are an index too, misspellings and all.
  5. Put what changed next to what didn't. What happened on one line; what was promised and paid on the other.
  6. Check the pattern before you believe it. One exciting spike turned out to be TV and copy-day dates. Say so.
  7. Include your own worst moments. A record that only shows one side isn't a record.
  8. Ask in writing, one question per office. Certified mail, a deadline, then the free advocate. Fewer phone calls, fewer triggers.
  9. Pace the machine and yourself. Queues that wait for free resources, a pause switch, handoff notes. No marathons.

Rules it runs on

Local, honest, and yours to share

  • Only your own devices. No one else's accounts or systems.
  • Local by default. Nothing uploads, emails or publishes by itself.
  • Honest by design. Speaker names are marked as automatic, low-confidence lines are flagged, and your own raised voice stays in.
  • Built with free tools. WhisperX, pyannote, Ollama, ffmpeg, adb, open models.
  • Check your state's recording law. This is a tool, not legal advice.

How the sausage was made (simple version)

  1. Phones in. USB-C cable, adb, pull only what's new. Phones used: a 2024 Moto G Play, two 2023 Moto G Stylus, a 2018 LG V35, plus their SD cards.
  2. Everything else in. Account exports for notes, calendar, texts and calls; email through the provider; paper scanned with a phone scanner app.
  3. Recording apps found: the phone's built-in Recorder, an SD-card "Recordings" folder, a stylus-phone audio recorder, and a quick voice-memo app.
  4. Fix dates, hash everything, then transcribe, separate voices, search by word and meaning, and cross-check against notes, calendar and trips.
  5. Write it up in tiers: one plain page for the family, a care page for the care team (with consent), a certified-mail packet for an advocate, the full index for a lawyer.

Planned: an official records export (or records request) from the care receiver's online records portal, to line trips up with hours. Screen automation (scrcpy) only as a last resort.

What it found (in general terms)

  • One company promised a care-hours increase; the other received a smaller number for the same start date.
  • A written request for a full audit was answered twice with the same portal sign-up flyer.
  • Payroll had no representative authorization on file; the family had handed it to the care-plan company.
  • A care-plan staffer said on the record there is no team that does audits.
  • The daily log runs about 14–15 hours; approved paid hours ran 3.5 to 5.25.
  • One "spike" in the data was TV and copy-day timestamps. It was reported as an artifact, not a finding.

Path to those findings

Keyword lists from the key meetings → the owner's own filename tags → meaning search → same-day cross-checks (notes, calendar, email, trips) → read every lead in context before calling it strong.

Chain of custody

  • Originals are never edited. Copies are made read-only and verified by size and SHA-256.
  • Every derived file (transcript, summary, index) is checksummed and traces back to its source hash.
  • Recording dates come from the file's own metadata, and the copy-day problem is documented.
  • Queue logs record what ran, when, and what failed or was retried.
  • Modeled on the principles of ISO/IEC 27037 (identify, collect, acquire, preserve) and NIST SP 800-86. Not a certification.

Personal and health information

  • All audio and text processing runs locally. Nothing is uploaded automatically.
  • Public outputs carry counts only, with generic labels (Company A/B, "care receiver", "local contacts").
  • Names, places and health details stay in the private record, shared only by the owner's choice.

Strategies learned

  • Your own filenames and misspellings are an index.
  • Count incidents, not mentions.
  • Check a pattern before you believe it.
  • Keep context words out of the transcriber (it can echo them back as fake lines).
  • Queue long files last, and wait for free GPU memory.
  • Letters, not calls: one question per office, certified mail, a deadline.

Time to process

The first full pass (about 750 hours of audio) took roughly a week of evenings and overnights on one 16 GB GPU. A deeper sweep of about 1,500 more recordings is queued behind it.

Context · self-directed services (SDS)

Who holds which piece of the record

In self-directed Medicaid long-term care, a person can hire their own caregiver, often a family member. The work runs through several separate organizations, and each keeps its own part of the record. This page names none of them; it describes roles only.

The member

The person receiving care. In the fiscal/employer agent model, the member is the legal employer of their caregiver.

The managed care organization (MCO)

Its care team assesses needs and authorizes services and hours. In Wisconsin Family Care, self-directed supports is an option inside that plan.

The fiscal employer agent (F/EA)

A financial management services provider that runs payroll, tax filing and timesheets for the member-employer, paid from the budget the MCO authorizes.

The county or benefits office

Handles eligibility and any monthly cost share, on its own records and its own appeal route.

The caregiver

Does the work and logs the hours, often in the payroll app, and ends up the only one holding all the pieces, mostly on their own phone.

The gap phone+net+tics fills

There is often no shared system joining these records. The caregiver's own dated, checksummed copy lets each written question name exactly which piece is missing, and from whom.
